Termites & Pests

The hidden threat

Of all the things that can derail a real estate transaction late in the process, few are as quietly destructive as termites and other wood-destroying organisms. They work invisibly, often for years, until structural damage becomes obvious — usually right after a home inspection. Understanding the risk, the treatment options, and the role of a termite bond is essential for anyone buying or selling a home.

$5+ billion
Estimated annual property damage caused by termites in the United States, according to the National Pest Management Association. Standard homeowners insurance does not cover it — the damage is considered preventable.

Termites in the United States

The United States is home to several termite species, each with its own habits and geographic range:

  • Subterranean termites are the most common and the most destructive. They build colonies in soil and tunnel up through mud tubes into a home's framing. Found in every state except Alaska.
  • Drywood termites live entirely inside wood, with no soil contact required. Most active in the southern coastal states from Florida to California.
  • Formosan termites are an aggressive invasive species, particularly damaging because of their massive colony sizes (millions of individuals). Concentrated in Louisiana, Florida, Texas, Mississippi, Alabama, Hawaii, and the Carolinas.
  • Dampwood termites require very moist wood and rarely infest sound homes. Found in the Pacific Northwest, parts of the Southwest, and Florida.

Where termites are most active

The USDA Forest Service publishes a Termite Infestation Probability (TIP) zone map that divides the country into four risk levels. Knowing your zone — and the zone of any home you're buying — should inform how aggressively you protect against termites.

TIP Zone 1 · Very heavy
Southeast & Gulf Coast
Florida, southern Georgia, southern Alabama, southern Mississippi, southern Louisiana, southern Texas, southern California, Hawaii. Year-round termite pressure.
TIP Zone 2 · Moderate to heavy
Mid-Atlantic & Mid-South
North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Arkansas, much of Virginia, southern Missouri, much of California and Arizona.
TIP Zone 3 · Slight to moderate
Lower Midwest & Mountain West
Kentucky, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Kansas, Utah, Colorado. Annual inspections still recommended.
TIP Zone 4 · None to slight
Northern states
Minnesota, Wisconsin, North Dakota, Maine, Vermont, and Alaska. Termite activity is rare but not impossible.

Termite bonds explained

A termite bond is a renewable contract with a licensed pest-control company that covers inspection, treatment, and (sometimes) repair of termite damage. Bonds come in two main flavors, and the difference is significant:

Retreatment bond

The pest-control company performs an initial treatment, inspects the home each year, and re-treats at no additional cost if termites are found. It does not cover the cost of repairing any damage. Typical cost: $300–$500 per year after the initial treatment.

Repair bond (full bond)

Includes everything in a retreatment bond plus coverage of the cost to repair new termite damage that occurs during the bond period. Typical cost: $500–$1,500 per year. Some bonds cap the repair coverage; read carefully.

Read the fine print. Most bonds exclude damage that existed before the bond was issued, and exclude damage caused by other wood-destroying organisms like carpenter ants or wood-decay fungus. Hidden moisture issues that voided the warranty are a common reason claims get denied.

The WDO report ("termite letter")

In most southeastern states, lenders require a Wood-Destroying Organism (WDO) inspection report — commonly called a "termite letter" — before they will close a loan. It's typically valid for 30–90 days, must be issued by a licensed pest inspector, and lists any active or past infestation along with conditions that could lead to one.

Who orders it depends on local custom and contract:

  • In many southeastern states, the seller traditionally pays for and provides the report.
  • In others, the buyer orders it as part of their inspection contingency.
  • VA and USDA loans almost always require a WDO report regardless of state custom.

If the report shows active infestation, the buyer or lender will typically require treatment (and sometimes repair) before closing, often paid by the seller.

Other pests worth knowing about

Termites are the headline threat, but they aren't the only one:

  • Carpenter ants don't eat wood like termites do, but they hollow it out to nest. Common in the Pacific Northwest and parts of the Northeast.
  • Powderpost beetles attack hardwood floors, beams, and furniture. Easy to miss because the only visible sign is tiny pinholes.
  • Wood-decay fungi ("wood rot") often go hand in hand with termite damage and indicate a chronic moisture problem.
  • Rodents rarely affect structural integrity but can chew electrical wiring, contaminate insulation, and create fire risk.
  • Bed bugs are typically a tenant problem in multi-unit buildings rather than a structural one, but they can complicate a transfer of occupancy.

What buyers should do

  • Always include a WDO inspection in your purchase contract, even in low-TIP states. The cost is $75–$200 and the protection is enormous.
  • Ask the seller whether the home has an active termite bond, and request a copy of it. Many bonds are transferable to the new owner for a small fee.
  • If you're buying in TIP Zone 1 or 2, budget for an annual bond from day one. It's standard practice in the Southeast.

What sellers should do

  • Have an annual inspection performed long before you list. Discovering active termites mid-transaction is the worst possible time.
  • If you have a transferable bond, gather the paperwork and recent inspection reports before showings begin.
  • If your home is in TIP Zone 1 or 2 and you've never had a bond, getting one before listing is a strong signal to buyers and removes a major closing risk.

Conclusion

Termites cause more annual property damage in the United States than fires, floods, and hurricanes combined — and unlike those threats, they're almost entirely preventable with a modest annual investment. For buyers, a $200 inspection and the right contingencies are a tiny price for the protection. For sellers, a clean, transferable termite bond is one of the simplest, highest-ROI things you can do to make a home easy to sell. Whether you're in the heart of the Southeast or in the colder northern states, knowing your zone and acting accordingly is the difference between a smooth closing and an expensive surprise.
