Discrimination in Real Estate

A history worth knowing

Real estate in the United States has been shaped by a long, painful history of discrimination, and the consequences of those practices are still felt today. Understanding this history is essential for any buyer or seller who wants to participate in a fair market — and to avoid, even unintentionally, perpetuating harm.

In the 1930s, the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation drew "residential security maps" of American cities, color-coding neighborhoods by perceived lending risk. Predominantly Black, Jewish, Asian, and immigrant neighborhoods were outlined in red and labeled "hazardous." The practice, which came to be called redlining, denied federally insured mortgages to families in those areas for decades — locking out entire communities from the central engine of American wealth building. A 2018 Federal Reserve study found that neighborhoods redlined in the 1930s still show lower homeownership rates, lower home values, and higher poverty rates today.

Redlining wasn't the only barrier:

  • Racial restrictive covenants — deed clauses forbidding the sale or rental of a home to Black, Jewish, Asian, or other minority buyers — were so common in 20th-century deeds that millions of U.S. properties still carry their original (now unenforceable) language.
  • Blockbusting, in which agents scared white owners into selling cheaply by warning that minority families were "moving in," then resold those homes at inflated prices to those same families, devastated wealth on both sides of the transaction.
  • Steering — directing buyers toward or away from neighborhoods on the basis of race, religion, or family composition — was widespread and is still documented in fair-housing audits today.

The Fair Housing Act and modern bias

The 1968 Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability. Many states and localities extend those protections to sexual orientation, gender identity, source of income, and other categories. But the law alone doesn't prevent bias. Discrimination today is often unintentional — driven by assumptions, the photos and stories attached to offer "love letters," or simple curiosity about who a buyer is.

Why offers must be judged on their merits

This is why purchase offers should be compared strictly on their merits. The information that matters is financial and contractual:

  • Offer price and how it compares to your asking price
  • Earnest money amount and timing
  • Financing type and the strength of the pre-approval letter
  • Contingencies (inspection, appraisal, financing, sale of current home)
  • Requested closing date and any post-occupancy needs
  • Inspection and repair terms

Information that should never factor into your decision includes the buyer's name, photo, race, religion, family makeup, sexual orientation, occupation, or any personal story they choose to share. Buyer "love letters," once popular, are now discouraged or outright banned in several states because they invite exactly this kind of bias.

How Abode helps. The offer dashboard intentionally hides buyer names and photos by default. For buyers and sellers today

If you're selling, the best protection — both for the people you're choosing between and for yourself — is structure. Receive offers in a format that highlights terms, not personalities. Compare them side by side on price and conditions. Ask your closing attorney or title agent whether any local rule applies to buyer communications.

If you're buying, knowing your rights matters. You cannot legally be steered, denied financing, or shown a smaller selection of homes because of who you are. If something feels off, you can file a complaint with HUD at hud.gov/fairhousing — every report adds to the public record.

Equal access to housing has been a slow, hard-won fight in America. Treating every offer like the financial document it is, and every buyer like the qualified counterparty they are, is one of the most practical things any participant in the market can do to keep that fight moving forward.
