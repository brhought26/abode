History of NAR

A trade group with extraordinary power

The National Association of Realtors (NAR) is the largest trade association in the United States, with roughly 1.5 million dues-paying members and more than a century of influence over how Americans buy and sell homes. Almost every major institution in residential real estate — from the multiple-listing services to the electronic lockboxes hanging on the front doors of homes for sale — is owned, controlled, or governed by NAR. For most of the 20th century, that institutional dominance gave NAR an unusual ability to shape the rules of a market it also served. The story of how that dominance was built — and how it is finally beginning to unwind — is essential context for anyone considering buying or selling a home outside the traditional agent model.

The birth and rise of the MLS monopoly

Multiple-listing services were created in the late 1800s as private agreements between real estate firms: I'll share my listings with you if you share yours with me. Over time, these informal networks consolidated into local boards, and the local boards consolidated under NAR's umbrella. By the second half of the 20th century, the MLS had become the definitive directory of homes for sale in every metropolitan area — and access to it was restricted to NAR members.

For most of the modern era, "listing on the MLS" was effectively synonymous with selling a home. A property not on the MLS was invisible to the buying public, because every search portal, every cooperating brokerage, and every consumer-facing website pulled its data from the MLS. If you wanted to sell, you needed an agent. If you wanted to be an agent, you needed to be a NAR member. That circular dependency is exactly what made the MLS so commercially powerful — and so legally contested.

How 6% became "standard"

For decades, the standard residential commission in the United States was 6% of the sale price, customarily split 3% to the listing agent and 3% to the buyer's agent. There has never been a statute or regulation establishing this. It became "standard" because NAR's MLS rules historically required sellers to offer a cooperating commission to the buyer's agent in order to list, because NAR's Code of Ethics, training materials, and customary practice all pointed toward the 5–6% range, and because brokerages and agents acted in remarkably consistent lockstep around those numbers.

The result was a commission structure remarkably resistant to market pressure. Agent commissions in the U.S. have remained roughly twice those of comparable services in most other developed countries, even as flat-fee brokerages, discount listing services, and FSBO platforms have spent decades trying to compete. Innovation kept coming. The pricing didn't budge. That is not how a competitive market is supposed to behave.

A legal track record worth knowing

NAR's commission practices have been challenged in court repeatedly — and the trajectory of those rulings has only been moving in one direction.

1950 United States v. National Association of Real Estate Boards

The U.S. Supreme Court ruled NAR's predecessor organization violated the Sherman Antitrust Act by publishing and enforcing fixed commission schedules. The decision established a precedent that has been cited in every major commission case since: uniform commission practices among ostensibly competing brokers can constitute illegal price-fixing.

2008 DOJ settlement on MLS access

The U.S. Department of Justice forced NAR to settle claims that its rules had been used to block "virtual office website" brokerages — early internet-based discount firms — from accessing MLS data on the same terms as traditional offices. The settlement opened a small crack in the gate.

2023–24 Sitzer / Burnett v. NAR

In October 2023, a federal jury in Missouri found NAR and the largest U.S. brokerages liable for conspiring to keep commissions artificially high. The jury awarded home-seller plaintiffs $1.78 billion in damages — an amount that could have been tripled under federal antitrust law. Rather than appeal and face an avalanche of additional class actions, NAR settled in March 2024 for $418 million and agreed to significant structural changes.

What changed in August 2024

The Sitzer/Burnett settlement reshaped the rules that had governed residential real estate for half a century. Effective August 17, 2024, NAR was required to:

  • Prohibit offers of compensation to buyer-side agents from appearing on the MLS — ending the practice that had previously made the 3% buyer-agent commission feel inevitable to sellers.
  • Require written buyer-broker agreements before any agent can show a home, so buyers have to explicitly agree to (and understand) what they're paying their agent and why.
  • Decouple seller and buyer commissions so each side independently negotiates with their own representation.

The practical result: sellers in 2026 no longer have a structural obligation to pay the buyer's agent. They can — many still do — but it is now an explicit negotiation rather than a hidden assumption baked into the listing process.

Lockboxes and electronic gatekeeping

Beyond the MLS itself, NAR has historically controlled the other piece of infrastructure that touches every transaction: the lockboxes hanging on every for-sale home in America. Two electronic systems — SentriLock and Supra — dominate the market. SentriLock has been a wholly-owned subsidiary of NAR since 2018.

Access to these systems is restricted to licensed agents who are also NAR members. The practical effect is that a self-represented seller could not, until very recently, easily provide secure key access to a self-represented buyer without going through a NAR-affiliated agent. That last layer of gatekeeping is now eroding too, as buyers and sellers turn to inexpensive smart locks, scheduled in-person showings, and platform-based scheduling tools that bypass the agent-controlled lockbox entirely.

The lobbying machine

NAR is consistently one of the largest political spenders in Washington. In recent years it has reported lobbying expenditures of over $80 million annually, regularly placing it in the top three of all trade associations and corporate spenders — alongside the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and major pharmaceutical groups. That spending has been deployed for decades to protect commission structures, defend the agent-centric MLS model, and oppose state-level proposals that would have made flat-fee or discount listing easier to scale.

Whether that lobbying represents legitimate industry advocacy or the protection of a regulated cartel has been debated for half a century. The recent class-action verdicts — multiple, in multiple states — suggest the courts have begun to reach an answer.

Why MLS doesn't matter the way it used to

Here is the structural reality of 2026: the practical purpose of the MLS — getting your home in front of buyers — has been substantially replaced by the public internet. Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com, Trulia, and dozens of regional portals all syndicate listings, and most accept FSBO submissions directly. A property listed on a single popular consumer portal will be seen by more potential buyers in 24 hours than a typical MLS listing reached in a month a generation ago. Buyers no longer find homes by calling agents and asking what's available — they find homes on their phones, schedule showings online, and ask agents only if they want one.

The MLS still matters at the margins, especially for compatibility with cooperating agents who serve buyers represented under traditional models. But for buyers and sellers transacting directly, the MLS has stopped being a gate that has to be passed. It's an option, not a requirement — and the courts have now twice ruled that pretending otherwise was the result of coordinated action.

Conclusion

The history of NAR is the history of a trade association that built and defended one of the longest-running quasi-monopolies in American consumer commerce. That history matters for anyone selling a home in 2026 not because it should make you avoid agents — many agents do excellent work and earn every dollar — but because it should give you the confidence that the alternative is real, legal, and increasingly normal. The system has been opening up. With the right tools, the right preparation, and the educational resources you'll find throughout this site, you can participate in your own transaction without paying the cost of an institutional structure the courts have now ruled was, for decades, kept artificially closed.
